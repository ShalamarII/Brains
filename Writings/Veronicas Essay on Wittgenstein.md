	Ludwig Wittgenstein begins Philosophical Investigations with an excerpt from Augustine’s Confessions with the purpose of critiquing the notion that all words are simply names of objects. While his critique of Augustine’s account of learning language is warranted, Wittgenstein fails to acknowledge the essence of human language that persists in Augustine’s quote. In this essay, I will highlight the essence of human language hidden in Augustine’s Confessions without disagreeing with Wittgenstein’s critique. In doing so, I will conclude that Augustine, although not explicitly, does portray features essential to human language, and that this ‘essence’ may be an answer to the dilemma Wittgenstein struggles with in §1.65.
	Wittgenstein claims that the “picture of the essence of human language[]” portrayed in Augustine’s excerpt is that “the individual words in language name objects—-where sentences are combinations of such names.(§1.1)” In claiming this to be the case, the ‘essence of human language’ dismisses words that don’t represent objects (for ex. The copula ‘is’ and tense markers), as ‘secondary’ or features that occur in ‘elevated’ (non-primitive) language. This is particularly evident in Augustine’s quote as he only mentions words for named objects as the words that he used “[...] to express [his] own desires.”, leaving “intention” within the domain of “[...] bodily movements, as it were the natural language of all peoples” 1 . Wittgenstein even goes so far as to cite this interpretation as the root of the idea that each word has a meaning that is 1:1 with an object(§1.1). While Wittgenstein’s interpretation and critiques of Augustine’s account are not inaccurate, it seems that there is indeed a more accurate portrayal of the essence of human language in Augustine’s quote.
	I believe Augustine was seeing a symptom of a greater underlying system that he had not yet noticed, and therefore of which he neglected more subtle symptoms. An example of a ‘neglected symptom’ being linguistic features that represent concepts such as time or preference, ironically partially appearing in Augustine’s account as “[...] the expression of the face, the play of the eyes, the movement of other parts of the body, and the tone of voice which expresses our state of mind in seeking, having, rejecting, or avoiding something. 2 ” Language is commonly misunderstood to refer to only oral productions, but the reality is that gestures, facial expression, and spatial displacement (amongst others) can be linguistic features, particularly once they’re adopted by a community with prototypical representations in mind (for example, how we now conceive of shrugs). 3
	Primitively, a language can appear to only consist of ‘content words’, but this is simply a symptom of using a shared reality, the physical plane, in order to communicate. The ‘essence’ of human language, underlies Augustine’s picture; it is the use of the shared physical plane in order to learn and teach (train) each other to communicate in ways that better convey what we mean to each other. This is in contrast to, for example, guessing intent based on physical avoidance (or lack thereof). Through the medium of the physical plane (and through our ability to share said plane), we are able to communicate. Initially through separate actions, then vague gestures, then sounds/gestures given arbitrary connections; as we train each other to recognize the arbitrary connections, we agree on them and begin to use them consistently for ease of communication. This gradual learned representation does not limit the human language to words that only represent objects, but it does show a preference for them, at least initially– that being said, even if the names of objects are easier to acquire than terms for concepts, ‘simple’ adjectives such as ‘hungry’ and ‘simple’ tenses such as the past can conceivably be expressed linguistically in the ‘early stages’ of human language all the same (regardless of if it is linguistically expressed through words (semantic meaning), word order (syntax), gestures, etc.) 4 . The initial preference for words that represent objects ‘directly’ can largely be attributed to the simplicity in which we can make it [more] obvious that we are referring to them. This is ignoring the fact that pointing, staring at, or even simply facing objects can make this evident in a linguistic (communicative), yet non-oral, way. 56 
	The primitive nature of a language that consists solely (or mostly) of just ‘content words’ (see: nouns) is necessarily an “in-between” step for human language, and is in this way ‘essential’; it is a foundational step in the process of developing human language in reality as we know it to exist. In the same way that people don’t tend to stop at dipping their brush in paint when their intent/motivation is to paint something 7 , human language doesn’t typically refrain from developing more robust syntax and varied vocabulary (outside of content words) when their intent/motivation is to convey something to others. As far as we can imagine, language has formed in order to communicate in more precise ways– without it, we can theoretically still communicate, but it would be much less rigorous and precise. But due to these motivations, it is theoretically necessary for the language to develop further; it is simply starting from assigning labels to objects as, not knowing the full extent of the objects, we conceive of prototypical objects easier than we would for prototypical actions, or prototypical states of mind 8.
	If we did not share the physical plane, yet still wanted to communicate, I imagine we’d have a different ‘essence’ of language; it would be hard, for example, to communicate anger solely through yelling 9– as another can interpret it as joy, or sorrow. We would then likely build off of the base expressions we have; to cry, in order to show a desire/need for something we lack. To giggle, in order to show something has brought us joy. (This is ignoring the fact that in this case we may not have the same bodies to giggle/cry with). But, to what extent would this be ‘innate’, in contrast to ‘trained’? 10 . Additionally, without a physical plane, the implications for human language could equally be that it would not exist (or at the very least, “human” would have to take on a wholly new definition).
	These conceptual differences in medium are, in a way, acknowledged in §1.19;" It is easy to imagine a language consisting only of orders and reports in battle.—Or a language consisting only of questions and expressions for answering yes and no. And innumerable others.- And to imagine a language means to imagine a form of life.”
	Different circumstances and motivations could converge in order to create these imagined languages. But it seems that, at least out of the languages mentioned above, every primitive language of humans would develop past that, as the motivation to communicate with each other can come from any multitude of variables; the fact that we can work together better when we communicate, that we can warn others when we communicate, that we can ask them for help. Without working together, it is harder (and lonelier) to survive. Even in the case where a language consisted only of orders and reports, it seems to me that more rigorous communication would emerge; see how pidgins become creoles. Even if the adults do not, children use the training they obtain and represent more facets of conception using the language training they obtain; it can be more useful to have more precise understanding, even if only in labour (it could lessen chances of punishment, for example, in a language where we only see ‘orders and reports’(§1.19)). It can be less lonely, and less hard, to have more precise ways to express and understand others. Certain patterns that are evident, can be easier to give labels to rather than to ignore the pattern altogether.
	In §1.65, Wittgenstein answers an objection (towards his lack of a distinct answer towards the ‘essence’ of language and the general form of a proposition) with the explanation that “I am saying that these phenomena have no one thing in common which makes us use the same word for all,— but that they are related to one another in many different ways.(§1.65)”. He goes on to illustrate a complicated network in which everything that is related, is related to different things in different ways– a lack of overarching similarity yet an interconnected web. My interpretation of the ‘essence’ of human language may provide the needed overarching similarity; As far as we know, humans are only able to connect and share things using reality/the ‘physical’ plane. If we were able to somehow exist past that plane, my theory could still be the case so long as there is some ability to connect using any medium. If we were unable to do so, if any language were to exist, it would not be human language. Human language appears to be predicated on the ability to act in a shared medium (in this case, the physical plane) and therefore any language necessarily exists in this context– therefore giving a common link to human language.
		Although Augustine’s explanation of the foundations of human language is not accurate, the essence of human language persists in his portrayal; human language exists through the ability to act in a shared medium (the physical plane) with the human inclination towards attempting to convey our thoughts and understanding others. This, to me, is the overarching link between all human language, even when the surface realization of language suggests a more direct relationship with reality (such as the notion that all words are names of objects).
